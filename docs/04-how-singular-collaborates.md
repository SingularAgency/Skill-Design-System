# How Singular collaborates

**Status:** v0.1 working reference

**Purpose:** Explain Singular's role between the client's operating problem and
a working, owned capability

Singular is not useful because it introduces AI into a company. It is useful
when it helps the company change how consequential work happens—and leaves the
people inside the company able to understand, control, and evolve that change.

## Singular's role in one sentence

> Singular is an outcome partner that helps an established SMB turn fragmented
> context and manual coordination into a governed operating capability the
> client can own and adopt.

This statement is a working strategic definition. It does not replace a legal
scope, service description, or approved commercial claim.

## What Singular is

Singular combines:

- operating problem framing;
- workflow and product design;
- shared context and business rules;
- human and agent execution;
- visible scope, evidence, and decisions;
- adoption and operating ownership.

The value is in the relationship between these parts. A workflow without
context is brittle. Automation without governance is risky. A product without
adoption becomes another unused system.

## What Singular is not

Singular should not present itself as:

- a generic AI consultancy;
- a chatbot or automation shop;
- a tool reseller;
- a dashboard vendor;
- an agency that owns the client's operating system;
- an autonomous system that removes all human judgment;
- a transformation program that must redesign the whole company before showing
  useful work.

## The collaboration model

The current working model follows seven decisions.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A["Frame the operating problem"] --> B["Choose one consequential workflow"]
    B --> C["Ground it in context, rules, and ownership"]
    C --> D["Design the capability and review model"]
    D --> E["Coordinate human and agent delivery"]
    E --> F["Prove behavior, evidence, and adoption"]
    F --> G["Transfer ownership and choose what scales next"]
```

This is a strategic model, not a fixed project plan or numeric delivery
promise.

### 1. Frame the operating problem

Begin with the way work fails:

- Where is context lost?
- What manual coordination repeats?
- Which decision returns to the same person?
- What business consequence is visible?
- What part of the problem is fact, inference, or unknown?

Do not begin by selecting a model, agent, or feature.

### 2. Choose one consequential workflow

The first scope should be narrow enough to understand and meaningful enough to
matter.

It needs:

- a workflow owner;
- an observable current state;
- a desired operating outcome;
- a defined decision or handoff;
- available context and systems;
- an evidence and adoption standard.

“Use AI across the company” is not a workable first scope.

### 3. Ground it in context, rules, and ownership

Map:

- live operating information;
- stable policies, terminology, and process rules;
- system and source boundaries;
- permissions;
- human judgment points;
- exceptions and escalation;
- who owns the capability after implementation.

This prevents a technically functional automation from contradicting the
company's operation.

### 4. Design the capability and review model

Define how the workflow should behave:

- event or input;
- interpretation;
- state and owner;
- agent or human action;
- evidence;
- decision gate;
- exception and recovery;
- next state.

The review model is part of the capability, not an approval screen added at the
end.

### 5. Coordinate human and agent delivery

Use the right actor for the work:

- agents can interpret, generate, organize, compare, and execute within defined
  boundaries;
- humans retain judgment for ambiguous, sensitive, novel, or high-consequence
  work;
- both need visible context, state, and evidence;
- handoffs must explain why the work moved and what the next actor needs.

The percentage of agent or human work is not a universal promise.

### 6. Prove behavior, evidence, and adoption

Evidence must answer more than “was it built?”

Ask:

- Does the workflow behave as intended?
- Can the target user complete the job?
- Are sources and decisions understandable?
- Are exceptions recoverable?
- Is the capability used in real work?
- Did the operating outcome change?

Delivery evidence, QA judgment, client acceptance, and business impact are
different proof layers.

### 7. Transfer ownership and choose what scales next

The client should understand and own the relevant:

- workflow;
- documentation;
- rules and terminology;
- prompts and automations;
- interfaces and code;
- decision and review model;
- operating history and evidence.

The next capability should be selected from observed value and remaining
bottlenecks, not from a preset list of AI features.

## The client–Singular trust contract

### Singular commits to

- name the real problem before selling a mechanism;
- separate fact, inference, risk, and unknown;
- make scope, ownership, and next decisions visible;
- expose agent sources and limitations when relevant;
- route high-risk work to meaningful human review;
- avoid claiming outcomes without confirmation;
- design for the client's long-term ownership and adoption.

### The client contributes

- access to workflow owners and relevant users;
- current operating context and constraints;
- source-system access appropriate to the scope;
- decisions about permissions and risk;
- review and acceptance;
- participation in adoption and outcome measurement.

This is a collaboration model, not contract language.

## How the Singular products support the collaboration

### Website, presentations, social, and email

Help the right buyer recognize the operating problem, understand the
foundation-first approach, evaluate evidence, and choose a next conversation.

They do not prove a claim merely by repeating it.

### Singular Stories / Singular Agile

They are the same product. Singular Stories is the current product-facing name;
Singular Agile may remain in existing marketing contexts.

The product connects:

- strategy and desired outcomes;
- Projects, Objectives, Key Results, and Key Projects;
- Sprints and atomic work;
- assignment and delivery;
- evidence and QA;
- client authorization and acceptance;
- notifications and selected commercial state.

Its core job is to make commitments and delivery inspectable across client and
Singular roles.

### Singular Approvals on iOS

Provides a focused client decision experience:

- Pre-Work authorization before execution;
- Active work visibility;
- Post-Work acceptance after QA;
- concise decision briefs;
- scoped Task selection;
- identity and biometric confirmation;
- evidence and next-state clarity.

Mobile convenience must not reduce decision quality.

### Singularity Studio

Lets a semi-technical client express product intent in natural language and
review how the system interprets and proposes change.

It makes visible:

- intent and scope;
- agent activity;
- impacted surfaces;
- preview;
- diff;
- sources;
- limitations and blockers;
- human handoff;
- the decision to continue, revise, hand off, or publish when supported.

High-risk work and external publication require explicit gates and
confirmation.

## Company Brain, Rivers, and Totems

These are optional metaphors for explaining the context layer:

- **Rivers:** live operating signals that keep changing.
- **Totems:** stable rules, definitions, standards, permissions, and
  institutional knowledge.
- **Company Brain:** the connected context that lets people and agents
  interpret live information through stable business rules.

They are useful when they reduce abstraction. They are not mandatory language,
a database definition, or proof that all context is complete.

## The outcome boundary

Singular can design and deliver a capability. A business outcome also depends
on:

- source quality;
- client decisions;
- employee adoption;
- exception handling;
- operating volume;
- time;
- factors outside the workflow.

Therefore:

- a delivered feature is not automatically an adopted capability;
- an adopted capability is not automatically a verified business result;
- a result for one client is not a universal promise.

## Downstream implications

This collaboration model requires experiences that:

- begin with the user's operating job;
- preserve context across handoffs;
- distinguish proposed, authorized, delivered, accepted, and published;
- expose evidence and uncertainty;
- keep human control meaningful;
- use calm, exact language;
- create a coherent identity across different surfaces without making them
  behave the same.

Those requirements become the
[Experience foundations](./05-experience-foundations.md).
